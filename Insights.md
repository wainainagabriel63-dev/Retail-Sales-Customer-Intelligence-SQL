
# Duka Smart Electronics Business Insights and Recommendations.

This analysis explores the sales performance, customer behaviour, product profitability and order trends of Duka Smart Electronics. The findings highlight areas where the business is performing well, as well as opportunities to improve profitability, retain customers and increase sales.

## 1. Accessories Are Contributing More to Profit Than Their Sales Suggest

### Finding (Q16, Q33, Q42)

Although accessories account for only **4.46% of total revenue (KSh 1,077,496)**, they generate the highest profit margin among all product categories at **42.39%**. The other four categories have considerably lower margins, ranging between 9.92% and 11.38%.

Accessories generated **KSh 456,746 in profit**, representing 15.8% of the company's total profit. Interestingly, this is almost the same profit generated by Home Appliances (KSh 458,034), despite Home Appliances bringing in 3.7 times more revenue.

This shows that the products generating the highest sales are not necessarily the ones delivering the highest profits. For example, Anker 20W chargers and Oraimo earbuds have profit margins of approximately 50%, while the Hotpoint Refrigerator has a margin of just 4%.

### Recommendation

Duka Smart Electronics should take advantage of the strong margins on accessories by encouraging customers to purchase them alongside larger products.

For example:
- Customers buying phones could be offered chargers or earbuds.
- Laptop buyers could be encouraged to purchase a mouse or keyboard.
- Customers purchasing televisions could be offered soundbars.

Accessories should also be positioned near checkout counters and included as suggestions during online checkout.

Even a small increase in accessory sales could significantly improve overall profitability without requiring the business to sell substantially more expensive products.


## 2. Heavy Discounting Is Reducing the Company's Profits

### Finding (Discount Band Analysis)

The analysis shows a clear relationship between discount levels and profit margins. As discounts increase, profitability drops considerably.

| Discount Band | Number of Orders | Profit Margin |
|:---|---:|---:|
| 0% | 306 | 17.5% |
| 5–10% | 231 | 9.6% |
| 15% | 52 | 2.5% |
| 20–25% | 45 | -4.7% |

Out of the 634 completed orders, **62 were sold below cost price**, resulting in a combined loss of KSh 141,979.

The business also gave up KSh 1,522,246 through discounts. This is equivalent to 52.7% of the total profit it actually made, which stood at KSh 2,890,922.

This is concerning because the company's overall profit margin is already relatively low at 11.97%. Offering a 20% discount on products with margins of only 5–12% can easily turn a profitable sale into a loss.

### Recommendation

The business should introduce clearer rules for offering discounts.

- Generally limit discounts to 10%.
- Require manager approval for discounts of 15% or more.
- Prevent products from being sold below their cost price.
- Avoid heavy discounts on products with already low profit margins.

Products such as the Hotpoint Refrigerator (4.0%), Samsung 55-inch TV (4.6%), Hisense 43-inch TV (5.3%) and iPhone 13 (6.2%) should receive particular attention.

Where customers need an incentive to complete a purchase, the business could offer a free or discounted accessory instead. This would provide additional value to customers without putting as much pressure on profit margins.



## 3. A Small Group of Customers Generates Most of the Company's Revenue

### Finding (Q24, Q27, Q41, Q45)

The customer analysis reveals that a relatively small group of buyers contributes a large share of the company's revenue.

The top 10 customers account for 29.8% of total revenue, while the top 20 contribute 51.6%. Looking at the wider customer base, the top 25% of buyers (33 customers) generate 69.2% of revenue.

The High Value customer group, defined as customers who have spent at least KSh 250,000, consists of only 34 customers. However, these customers contribute KSh 16.97 million, representing 70.3% of total revenue.

On the other hand, the bottom 25% of customers contribute only 1.9% of revenue.

Another interesting finding is that the existing customer labels do not always reflect how much customers actually spend.

Nine of the top 10 highest-spending customers are classified as Retail customers. Similarly, 17 of the 34 High Value customers fall under the Retail category. Only 9 of the 19 customers labelled VIP are classified as High Value.

For example, the company's highest-spending customer, **Brian Chebet**, has spent KSh 848,119 but is classified as a Retail customer.

VIP customers do spend more on average (KSh 253,252 compared with KSh 163,575 for Retail customers, according to Q27). However, the current classification system fails to identify several other valuable customers.

### Recommendation

The business should consider grouping customers according to their actual spending rather than relying mainly on existing customer labels.

The spending categories identified in Q45 provide a useful starting point:

| Customer Segment | Spending Range |
|:---|:---|
| High Value | KSh 250,000 and above |
| Medium Value | KSh 60,000–249,999 |
| Low Value | Below KSh 60,000 |

High Value customers should receive more personalised attention. This could include a dedicated contact person, early access to new stock and priority delivery services.

The customer categories should also be reviewed every quarter to ensure they reflect current buying behaviour.

Retaining these customers is particularly important because losing even one major buyer could have a noticeable effect on the company's revenue.



## 4. More Than a Third of Customers Have Stopped Buying

### Finding (Q34)

The analysis shows that **47 out of the 132 customers who have previously purchased from the business (35.6%)** have not completed an order in more than 90 days.

Even more concerning, 31 of these customers have been inactive for over 180 days.

Together, these inactive customers have previously spent KSh 4,595,913, accounting for 19.0% of the company's total revenue.

The group also includes:
- 6 VIP customers with lifetime spending of KSh 1.37 million.
- 7 Corporate customers who have spent KSh 0.82 million.
- 6 customers previously classified as High Value.

Some customers have been inactive for a particularly long time. For instance, **Agnes Kimani** last completed an order on 13 January 2025, meaning she had been inactive for 625 days by the end of the dataset.

These figures suggest that the business may be missing opportunities to generate more sales from customers who have already purchased its products.

### Recommendation

Duka Smart Electronics should introduce a customer re-engagement campaign targeting buyers who have not made a purchase recently.

The business should prioritise customers in the following order:

1. Inactive High Value customers.
2. Inactive VIP customers.
3. Inactive Corporate customers.
4. Other inactive customers.

For the most valuable customers, a personal phone call or WhatsApp message would be more appropriate than a general promotional message.

Other customers could receive targeted offers through WhatsApp or SMS, including promotions that make it easy to pay through M-Pesa.

The business should avoid using large discounts to attract these customers back, especially given the effect discounts already have on profitability. Offering a free accessory or a small additional benefit could be a more affordable way to encourage repeat purchases.

The campaign should be monitored over a 30-day period to establish how many inactive customers return and make another purchase.



## 5. Sales Are Growing, but the Business Needs to Prepare for Seasonal Changes

### Finding (Q31, Q38–Q40, Q43)

The business has recorded strong revenue growth in 2026.

Between January and September 2026, total revenue reached **KSh 12.83 million**, representing an 86.3% increase compared with the same period in 2025, when revenue stood at KSh 6.89 million.

The third quarter also performed well. Revenue increased from KSh 3.26 million in Q3 2025 to KSh 5.86 million in Q3 2026, representing growth of 79.8%.

Monthly revenue has also increased considerably, moving from KSh 434,903 in January 2025 to KSh 2,092,445 in September 2026.

The strongest months in the dataset were:

| Month | Revenue |
|:---|---:|
| August 2026 | KSh 2,242,034 |
| September 2026 | KSh 2,092,445 |
| June 2026 | KSh 2,038,372 |

However, sales have not increased consistently every month.

December 2025 recorded revenue of KSh 2,027,121 from 69 orders, an increase of 59.7% compared with November.

This was followed by a sharp decline in January 2026, when revenue dropped by 84.1% to KSh 322,433. January was the weakest month in the entire dataset, with only 25 completed orders.

There were also smaller declines during May and July 2026, when revenue fell by 28.8% and 25.1%, respectively, before recovering.

### Recommendation

The business should use its sales history to prepare for periods of high and low demand.

Stock levels and staffing should gradually increase from November to ensure the business can handle the expected rise in December sales.

At the same time, management should prepare a January marketing campaign to encourage customers to continue shopping after the festive season.

Promotions could focus on:
- Laptops and monitors for customers preparing for work or school.
- Affordable accessory bundles.
- Product combinations that offer additional value without significantly reducing prices.

Rather than relying on price reductions, the business should focus on offers that maintain healthy profit margins.

Monthly sales should also be monitored closely so that unexpected declines, such as those experienced in May and July 2026, can be identified and addressed early.



## 6. Nairobi Leads in Revenue, but Other Towns Offer Room for Growth

### Finding (Q17, Q25)

Nairobi is currently the company's largest market, contributing **39.7% of total revenue**, equivalent to KSh 9.58 million.

It accounts for 48 of the 150 customers, or approximately 32% of the customer base. On average, each buying customer in Nairobi spends KSh 233,707.

Mombasa comes second, generating KSh 3.16 million, with an average spending level of KSh 210,793 per buyer.

However, the analysis also highlights opportunities in smaller markets.

Machakos, for example, has only three customers, but their average spending is KSh 248,367 per buyer. This is the highest among the towns analysed and is above Nairobi's average. The town contributes KSh 745,100 in total revenue.

On the other hand, Thika and Meru record lower spending per buyer, at KSh 116,358 and KSh 136,733, respectively.

Thika's performance is particularly worth examining because of its proximity to Nairobi, yet its customers spend roughly half as much per buyer.

### Recommendation

The business should explore opportunities to attract more customers in towns where spending per buyer is already relatively high.

Machakos and Mombasa would be suitable markets for targeted marketing campaigns and customer referral programmes.

In Thika and Meru, the focus could be on encouraging customers to purchase higher-value products and complementary accessories. Bundling related products could help increase the average amount spent per customer.

Nairobi should remain a major priority because it contributes such a large share of total revenue. Maintaining good customer service, sufficient stock and reliable delivery in this market is essential, since any major disruption could affect a significant portion of the company's sales.



## 7. Pending Orders and Cancellations Represent Missed Sales Opportunities

### Finding (Q20, Q12)

The order analysis shows that cancellations and pending transactions are affecting the business's ability to convert orders into completed sales.

Out of 800 recorded orders, **90 were cancelled**, giving a cancellation rate of 11.25%. These orders had a combined value of KSh 3,465,356 based on their recorded order prices.

There are also 76 orders worth KSh 3,178,652 that remain Pending. This amount represents a further 13.2% of completed revenue that has not yet been realised.

Cancellation rates also vary depending on the payment method used.

| Payment Method | Number of Orders | Cancellation Rate |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Bank Transfer | 84 | 4.8% |
| M-Pesa | 441 | 12.5% |
| Cash | 124 | 12.1% |

Looking at product categories, Accessories have the highest cancellation rate at 13.1%, closely followed by Mobile Phones at 13.0%. Electronics have the lowest rate at 8.6%.

### Recommendation

The business should make it a routine practice to follow up on pending orders at least once a week.

Customers could receive a WhatsApp reminder or an M-Pesa payment prompt to encourage them to complete their purchases.

Management should also investigate why M-Pesa orders have a higher cancellation rate than bank transfers. Failed or expired payment requests could be one possible explanation, but the payment records would need to be reviewed before drawing a conclusion.

For expensive products, the business could consider requesting a small deposit before reserving stock. Customers could also be given the option of paying through bank transfer where appropriate.

However, the bank transfer figures are based on a smaller number of orders, so the business should test this approach before making it a standard policy.

Reducing cancelled orders and following up on pending transactions would help the company improve its order completion rate and make better use of available stock.




## Overall Conclusion

The findings show that Duka Smart Electronics has a good opportunity to improve its performance by making better use of the customers and products it already has.

Although revenue has grown significantly, the business is still losing potential profit through excessive discounts, missed accessory sales and inactive customers.

Rather than focusing only on increasing sales volume, management should pay closer attention to profitability, customer retention and order completion.

Taking action in these areas would help the business turn its strong sales performance into more sustainable and consistent profits.
